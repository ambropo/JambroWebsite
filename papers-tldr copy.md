# Papers — Titles and TL;DRs

## Working Papers

**Industrial Policies, Global Imbalances and Technological Hegemony**
with Andrea Ferrero, Luca Fornaro & Martin Wolf · January 2026
TL;DR: High-tech industrial policy can deliver technological hegemony, running persistent trade surpluses that erode rivals' innovation — unless they respond in kind.

**Monopsony, Income Risk and R\* Multiplicity**
with Sergio de Ferra, Andrea Ferrero, Alexandre Kohlhas, Michael McMahon, Federica Romei & Giovanni Rosso · August 2025
TL;DR: When corporate debt amplifies workers' income risk, the natural rate of interest can settle at two levels — and QE or public debt decides which one prevails.

**Capital Flows and Exchange Rates: A Quantitative Assessment of the Dilemma Hypothesis** (R&R · AEJ: Macroeconomics)
with Andrea Ferrero & Shangshang Li · August 2025
TL;DR: When the Fed tightens, small economies' exports and GDP fall even as their currencies depreciate — floating rates don't insulate them, and capital-flow and credit tools cushion output but not inflation.

**Dash for Dollars** (R&R · Review of Financial Studies)
with Robert Czech & Fernando Eguren-Martin · May 2025
TL;DR: In a crisis, investors flee specifically into US dollars — dumping dollar bonds far harder than other-currency bonds of the same firm to meet dollar funding needs.

**Dollar Shortages and Central Bank Swap Lines**
with Fernando Eguren-Martin, Andrea Ferrero & Will Pagel · new draft available soon
TL;DR: High-frequency swap-line shocks show that Fed dollar swap lines appreciate recipient currencies, lift equities, and ease the credit stress of the dollar shortages that recur in every global crisis.

**The Transmission of Keynesian Supply Shocks**
with Andrea Ferrero · August 2021
TL;DR: Some shocks that look like aggregate demand shocks are really sectoral supply shocks: when sectors are complementary, a supply shock in one travels through the production network and moves aggregate output and prices together, mimicking a demand shock.

**Global R\***
with Rich Harrison & Rana Sajedi · September 2024
TL;DR: The world's natural interest rate has fallen by about three percentage points since the mid-1970s, driven mainly by slowing productivity growth and rising longevity.

**Towards a New Monetary Theory of Exchange Rate Determination**
with Michael Kumhof, Andrej Sokol & Gregory Thwaites · August 2019
TL;DR: Exchange rates are driven less by interest-rate gaps than by banks creating money.

## Publications

**The Heterogeneous Effects of Carbon Pricing: Macro and Micro Evidence**
*Journal of the European Economic Association*, conditionally accepted — with Brendan Berthold, Federico di Pace & Alex Haberis
TL;DR: Carbon pricing raises inflation and cools activity, hitting carbon-intensive ("brown") firms and countries hardest — though general-equilibrium adjustments attenuate the aggregate impact.

**Crossing the Credit Channel: Credit Spreads and Firm Heterogeneity**
*American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 16(3), July 2024, 417–446 — with Gareth Anderson
TL;DR: When monetary policy tightens, highly leveraged firms see borrowing costs jump far more than low-leverage ones — driven mainly by the risk premium investors demand, not by greater default risk.

**Financial Shocks, Credit Spreads, and the International Credit Channel**
*Journal of International Economics* 135, March 2022 — with Andrej Sokol
TL;DR: US financial shocks spill abroad fast: tighter US credit conditions quickly raise foreign credit spreads and cool activity, even when local policy eases — an international credit channel.

**Uncertainty and Economic Activity: A Multi-Country Perspective**
*Review of Financial Studies* 33(8), August 2020, 3393–3445 — with M. Hashem Pesaran & Alessandro Rebucci
TL;DR: A common global factor accounts for most of the negative link between volatility and growth; once you control for it, the correlation nearly vanishes — so single-country studies overstate uncertainty's punch.

**Monetary Policy Transmission in the United Kingdom: A High-Frequency Identification Approach**
*European Economic Review* 123, April 2020 — with Gregory Thwaites & Alejandro Vicondoa
TL;DR: Using the first high-frequency UK rate surprises, a monetary tightening cools output and prices, lifts the pound, and widens credit spreads at home and abroad.

**Finance and Synchronization**
*Journal of International Economics* 116, January 2019, 74–87 — with Jean Imbs & Jumana Saleheen
TL;DR: Financial integration doesn't simply raise or lower business-cycle synchronisation — it synchronises economies after country-specific shocks but desynchronises them after common ones.

**Foreign Booms, Domestic Busts: The International Dimension of Banking Crises**
*Journal of Financial Intermediation* 37, January 2019, 58–74 — with Fernando Eguren-Martin & Gregory Thwaites
TL;DR: Banking crises ride in from abroad — booming credit in the rest of the world predicts a crisis at home, especially in financially open economies.

**International Credit Supply Shocks**
*Journal of International Economics* 112, 2018, 219–237 — with Andrea Ferrero & Alessandro Rebucci
TL;DR: When global banks lever up and push credit across borders, rising house prices and stronger currencies inflate collateral and supercharge the boom.

**Uncertainty, Financial Frictions, and Nominal Rigidities: A Quantitative Investigation**
*Journal of Money, Credit and Banking* 50(4), June 2018, 603–636 — with Emilio Fernandez-Corugedo
TL;DR: Spikes in firm-level uncertainty hit output harder than economy-wide uncertainty, yet even together they explain only a modest slice of business-cycle swings.

**Does Easing Monetary Policy Increase Financial Instability?**
*Journal of Financial Stability* 30, June 2017, 111–125 — with Alessandro Rebucci
TL;DR: When bank lending rates are sticky, interest-rate policy alone can't secure both macroeconomic and financial stability — so it needs a macroprudential partner, such as a tax on household credit.

**Global Liquidity, House Prices, and the Macroeconomy: Evidence from Advanced and Emerging Economies**
*Journal of Money, Credit and Banking* 47(S1), March–April 2015 — with Luis Felipe Cespedes & Alessandro Rebucci
TL;DR: When global credit surges, house prices and spending jump far more in emerging economies than rich ones — housing collateral drives the rich world, exchange rates the emerging one.

**Housing Cycles and Macroeconomic Fluctuations: A Global Perspective**
*Journal of International Money and Finance* 37, October 2013, 215–238
TL;DR: A U.S. housing slump spills strongly into other advanced economies but barely dents major emerging ones, which prove surprisingly resilient to American shocks.

**China's Emergence in the World Economy and Business Cycles in Latin America**
*Economía* 12(2), Brookings Institution Press, 1–75 — with M. Hashem Pesaran, Alessandro Rebucci & TengTeng Xu
TL;DR: As trade ties shifted, China's pull on Latin American growth tripled while the US's halved — rewiring which giant drives the region's business cycle.

## Policy Writings

**What's the Effect of AI on r\*?**
*Policy panel, Bank of England conference on Transformative Artificial Intelligence*, 2026
TL;DR: It depends.

**Did supply constraints tilt the Phillips Curve?**
*Bank Underground*, 2023 — with Edward Hall, Marco Pinchetti and Julian Reynolds
TL;DR: Global supply-chain constraints steepen the Phillips Curve, making inflation more responsive to demand when supply is tight.

**Fed Tightening and International Spillovers**
*Policy panel, SoFiE Annual Conference*, 2023
TL;DR: The Fed's rapid, globally synchronised tightening spills abroad mainly through the global financial cycle and the dollar's dominance — not expenditure switching — strengthening the case for independent financial-stability tools.

**Tradable cost shocks and non-tradable inflation: real wages and spillovers**
*Bank Underground*, 2023 — with Federico Di Pace, Aydan Dogan and Alex Haberis
TL;DR: Energy-price shocks spread inflation from tradable goods to non-tradable services through real-wage resistance, without necessarily signalling de-anchored expectations.

**Structural change, global R\* and the missing-investment puzzle**
*Bank of England Staff Working Paper*, 2022 — with Andrew Bailey, Marco Garofalo, Richard Harrison, Nick McLaren, Sophie Piton and Rana Sajedi
TL;DR: The secular fall in global R\* is driven mainly by slower productivity growth and ageing, while a shift towards intangible capital explains the weakness in investment.

**No economy is an island: How foreign shocks affect UK macrofinancial stability**
*Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin*, 2021 — with Rosie Dickinson, Sevim Kösem, Simon Lloyd and Ed Manuel
TL;DR: Developments overseas account for about half the variation in UK activity and nearly all the variation in UK financial conditions, with outsized effects on tail risks.

**I need a dollar, dollar, a dollar is what I need**
*Bank Underground*, 2021 — with Fernando Eguren-Martin
TL;DR: In financial crises, investors rush specifically for US-dollar cash rather than other currencies, reflecting the dollar's dominant role in global finance.

**Can sectoral supply shocks have aggregate demand consequences?**
*Bank Underground*, 2021 — with Andrea Ferrero
TL;DR: Sector-specific supply shocks can spill over into economy-wide demand effects when sectors are complementary, amplifying their impact on output and prices.

**Equity market volatility and global growth expectations**
*VoxEU*, 2017 — with M. Hashem Pesaran and Alessandro Rebucci
TL;DR: Rising world-growth expectations explain part, but not all, of the 2016–17 fall in equity-market volatility, hinting at optimism that could later reverse.

**Foreign booms, domestic busts: the global dimension of banking crises**
*Bank Underground*, 2017 — with Fernando Eguren-Martin and Gregory Thwaites
TL;DR: Foreign credit booms strongly predict domestic banking crises, with the link running through financial channels rather than trade.

**International business cycle synchronisation: The role of financial linkages**
*VoxEU*, 2016 — with Jean Imbs and Jumana Saleheen
TL;DR: Financial integration synchronises economies hit by country-specific shocks but desynchronises them after common shocks — so its effect on business cycles depends on the shock.

**How could a shock to growth in China affect growth in the United Kingdom?**
*Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin*, 2016 — with Kate Stratford
TL;DR: A 1% slowdown in Chinese growth would cut UK GDP by about 0.1%, mostly indirectly via the UK's main trading partners rather than direct links.

**Will a Fed rate hike affect house prices in emerging markets?**
*Bank Underground*, 2015 — with Alessandro Rebucci
TL;DR: A 10% fall in cross-border credit lowers house prices by under 1% in advanced economies but over 3% in emerging markets.

**Is the Federal Reserve breeding the next financial crisis?**
*VoxEU*, 2013 — with Alessandro Rebucci
TL;DR: Prolonged loose US monetary policy can fuel financial imbalances, so an accommodative Fed risks sowing the conditions for the next crisis.

**The ugly truth about a renminbi revaluation for Latin America**
*VoxEU*, 2010 — with M. Hashem Pesaran, Alessandro Rebucci, Cesar Tamayo and TengTeng Xu
TL;DR: A renminbi revaluation is no cure-all for Latin America: a 10% revaluation would cut Chinese growth by ~0.5%, US growth by ~0.15% and Latin American growth by ~0.3%.
